Whisper can now write isiXhosa

We trained whisper-small for 2 hours on 51 hours of isiXhosa speech from 195 people. Then we tested it on 2,770 clips from 8 people it had never heard. It now gets about 97 letters in 100 right, and two out of three clips come out exactly right. On sentences it never practised, it is good but clearly weaker, and that is the number to quote.

The language tag fixed the chaos: no more guessing 52 languages or writing in Arabic script. That took letter errors from 75% to 46%. Training taught it isiXhosa, from 46% down to 3%. The tag was a 30-point shortcut; training did the other 43 points.

The honest number: sentences it never practised

NCHLT reuses its prompts: 82% of the test clips are sentences that other people also read in the training data. The model has never heard these speakers, but it has seen most of these sentences. So we score the two groups separately.

What its mistakes look like now

983 of the 2,770 clips still have at least one error. Of those:

720are one small slip (under 10% of letters), like umnumzana → umnomzana or kwinkcazo → kwingcazo.
34are only word boundaries: every letter right, but split or joined in the wrong place.
229are bigger misses. The worst are often unusual prompts that mix in English (kwe draw nganye, art ubethe zesize), or rare long words.

By speaker, letter errors range from 0.6% (speaker 503) to 6.9% (speaker 505), and women and men score about the same (3.2% vs 2.8%). Speaker 505 was also among the hardest for stock Whisper, so it's worth a listen.

How training went

This is the early edge of overfitting: the model gets much better on clips it has heard twice, but only a little better on new voices. Three passes is about the limit for this data. More training wouldn't help much; more varied data would. That is the first idea for stage 3.

What this means

Good: on clean, read isiXhosa from new speakers, the model writes correct isiXhosa, clicks and all, most of the time. For a 244-million-parameter model, 2 hours of training and about $2, that is a strong result.

Not yet proven: NCHLT is short prompts read into a phone. Conversation, noise, long recordings and mixed English are all harder, and we haven't tested any of them yet. The unseen-sentence score (6.2% / 36.6%) is the closest we have to "new isiXhosa", and it shows room to grow.

Stage 3: one change per run

Each one is measured on the same test, with the unseen-sentence score as the headline. My suggested order:

1Add FLEURS (full natural sentences, new speakers). Targets the unseen-sentence gap and overfitting. ~$2.
2Bigger model (whisper-medium, 3× the size). Usually more accurate, ~2–3× the time. ~$5.
3A real isiXhosa tag instead of borrowing Swahili's. Cleaner for the model card.
4Audio augmentation (noise, speed changes) for messier real-world recordings.
5Compare with Meta's MMS, an outside model that already supports isiXhosa, as a reality check.
