## 1. Executive Summary

- `[INFERENCE]` **Answer to Q1 (What problem is this really addressing?):** As a learning project, it succeeded completely; as a real-world system, the gap it actually addresses in 2026 is not basic read-speech isiXhosa transcription (commercial APIs and Meta's open 2025 Omnilingual ASR already exist), but rather **offline, openly licensed, small-footprint transcription of everyday South African isiXhosa-English code-switched speech** (speech mixing both languages in the same sentence).
- `[INFERENCE]` **Answer to Q2 (Was the approach right?):** Fine-tuning OpenAI Whisper with full parameter updates and strict experimental hygiene was an excellent choice for learning and for shipping a free browser demo, and data quality drove surprisingly strong monolingual scores; however, Whisper's fixed 30-second encoder, high token count per isiXhosa word, autoregressive looping (getting stuck repeating words), and single forced language tag (`<|sw|>`) make it structurally flawed for a fast, reliable, code-switched product.
- `[INFERENCE]` **Answer to Q3 (What concrete steps improve it most reliably now?):** Before spending another dollar on training, add standard Whisper anti-looping and voice-activity detection (VAD, stripping silence before transcription) guards (`$0`, likely unlocking the already-trained R-0029 checkpoint immediately); next, benchmark Meta's Apache 2.0 `omniASR_CTC` v2 checkpoints (frame-by-frame recognizers with no 30-second padding) on Swivuriso and Soapies (`~$0.50`); then fix training under-budgeting (1.30 epochs, meaning full passes through the training set, in R-0029), NCHLT-biased checkpoint selection, and missing SpecAugment (masking random parts of the sound image during training) (`~$8-12`).
- `[INFERENCE]` **Answer to Q4 (Is there a fundamentally better approach?):** Yes, two complementary step-changes exist: (1) switching the small edge architecture to an African-pretrained, variable-length CTC (Connectionist Temporal Classification) or Transducer encoder (such as Meta `omniASR_CTC_300M_v2` / `1B_v2` or `w2v-bert-2.0` 600M) paired with an external code-switched language model for 2-4x faster CPU speed and zero repetition loops, and (2) using a large teacher (`omniASR_LLM_7B_v2`) to pseudo-label (automatically transcribe and confidence-filter) consented/open conversational speech and sister Nguni data (isiZulu/isiNdebele) before distilling (training a smaller model to copy the teacher) down to an edge student.
- `[SOURCE]` **Critical fact-check of `SPEC.html`:** `SPEC.html` repeated ElevenLabs' generic landing-page banner ("3.1% WER on FLEURS", where WER is Word Error Rate, the percentage of wrong words), but ElevenLabs' own Xhosa benchmark table on that exact same webpage reports **39.7% WER for Scribe v1** and **38.0% WER for Gemini Flash 2** on FLEURS (vs 93.5% for Whisper Large v3).

## 2. Question 1: What Problem Is This Project Really Trying to Address?

### 2.1 The stated goal vs the underlying real-world problem

`[REPO]` According to `SPEC.html`, `AGENTS.md`, and `decisions.md` (D-0001, D-0002), the explicit primary goal of this project is educational: Ntlakanipho Mgaguli (a final-year Computer Engineering student at CPUT Bellville and a native isiXhosa speaker, D-0003) wanted to "learn something hard" in AI by directing coding agents end-to-end to fine-tune OpenAI Whisper for isiXhosa, specialise it for isiXhosa-English code-switched speech (Stage 4), and ship a public model, browser demo, and technical write-up on roughly $15-30 of AWS credits.

`[INFERENCE]` That learning goal is completely distinct from the real-world engineering problem of isiXhosa Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR, software that converts spoken audio into written text). Evaluating the project honestly requires separating "Did this teach modern speech AI well?" (yes, exceptionally well) from "Who in the real world needs isiXhosa ASR, what exists for them today, and what is still missing?"

### 2.2 Who needs isiXhosa speech recognition, and for what?

`[SOURCE]` According to Statistics South Africa's official *Census 2022 Statistical Release* (Report P0301.4, Tables 2.1, 2.8, and 2.9), South Africa's total population in 2022 was **62,027,503**, and **isiXhosa** was the language spoken most often in the household by **16.3%** of the population aged 1 and older (second only to isiZulu at 24.4%, and nearly double English at 8.7%). At province level, isiXhosa is the primary household language for **81.8%** of the Eastern Cape (7,230,204 residents) and **31.4%** of the Western Cape (7,433,019 residents, where CPUT Bellville is located).

`[INFERENCE]` Multiplying 16.3% by roughly 62 million yields approximately **10 million home-language isiXhosa speakers** (slightly less if excluding infants under age 1, per the table footnote, so ~9.8-10.1 million). This is a larger first-language population than several European languages that receive first-class commercial ASR support (for example, Swedish, Danish, or Czech).

`[SOURCE]` Both the Swivuriso dataset paper (Marivate et al., 2025/2026, `arXiv:2512.02201`) and South African commercial startup Lelapa AI (`lelapa.ai`) identify concrete domains where South African indigenous-language ASR is needed: healthcare and agricultural extension (explicit core domains of Swivuriso), financial services and telecom contact centres (Lelapa AI's commercial focus), civic/public services, and education.

`[SOURCE]` In addition, South African speech researchers at Stellenbosch University (van der Westhuizen & Niesler, LREC 2018; Biswas et al., LREC 2020, `arXiv:2003.03135`) and Stanford (Ògúnrèmí, Manning & Jurafsky, 2023, `arXiv:2311.15077`) document that everyday urban South African speech is heavily **code-switched**: speakers switch between isiXhosa and English both between sentences and inside single sentences, including attaching isiXhosa prefixes and suffixes directly onto English root words (intra-word switching, e.g., `i-grapefruit` or `ujoine`).

`[REPO]` In the project itself, real user needs surfaced organically: transcribing WhatsApp voice notes, tutoring and lecture explanations in mixed isiXhosa-English (`SPEC.html`), subtitling social media videos (`TODO.md`), and acting as an offline front-end recognizer inside a peer's hardware speech translator, Siyamthanda (`JOURNAL.md`, R-0032).

### 2.3 What already exists today, and what is genuinely missing?

`[SOURCE]` Commercial cloud coverage for isiXhosa (`xh-ZA`) in late 2026 is uneven and closed-source:
- `[SOURCE]` **Microsoft Azure AI Speech** explicitly supports `xh-ZA` (Xhosa, South Africa) for speech-to-text (`learn.microsoft.com` language support table).
- `[SOURCE]` **Google Cloud Speech-to-Text V2** explicitly supports `xh-ZA` on its `chirp_2` foundation model (with automatic punctuation, model adaptation, and word-level confidence, per `docs.cloud.google.com`).
- `[SOURCE]` **Amazon Transcribe (AWS)** supports South African English (`en-ZA`), Afrikaans (`af-ZA`), and isiZulu (`zu-ZA`), but **does not list isiXhosa (`xh-ZA`) at all** (`docs.aws.amazon.com`).
- `[SOURCE]` **ElevenLabs Scribe** offers paid `xh` transcription (`elevenlabs.io/speech-to-text/xhosa`). Crucially, while its marketing header claims "3.1% WER on FLEURS" (a generic cross-language headline that `SPEC.html` accidentally repeated as if it were Xhosa), the actual **Xhosa Transcription Benchmark** table on that same ElevenLabs page reports **39.7% WER for Scribe v1** and **38.0% WER for Gemini Flash 2** on FLEURS, against **93.5% WER for Whisper Large v3** and **99.9% WER for Deepgram Nova 2**.
- `[SOURCE]` **Lelapa AI (Vulavula)** builds African-language speech-to-text for telecom and financial contact centres (`lelapa.ai`, `docs.lelapa.ai`), highlighting isiZulu, Sesotho, Afrikaans, South African English, and African French on its public documentation home page; its public docs do not publish isiXhosa STT accuracy numbers.

`[SOURCE]` Open-weight ASR coverage changed dramatically between 2023 and 2026:
- `[SOURCE]` **OpenAI Whisper** (Radford et al., 2022, `arXiv:2212.04356`) covers 99 languages (English plus 96-98 others depending on version), but **omits isiXhosa**.
- `[SOURCE]` **NVIDIA NeMo open models** (`nvidia/canary-1b-v2` and `nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3`, CC-BY-4.0) cover 25 European languages and **zero African languages**.
- `[SOURCE]` **Meta MMS-1B** (Pratap et al., 2023, `arXiv:2305.13516`; `facebook/mms-1b-all`) supports 1,100+ languages including isiXhosa, reaching **6.1% CER** on FLEURS Xhosa test with a FLEURS-trained language model (Table A3), **but is released under CC-BY-NC-4.0 (non-commercial only)**.
- `[SOURCE]` **Meta Omnilingual ASR** (Omnilingual ASR team, Nov 2025, `arXiv:2511.09690`; `github.com/facebookresearch/omnilingual-asr`) is **Apache 2.0** (commercially permissive), covers 1,600+ languages including `xho_Latn` trained on **598.8 hours of isiXhosa** (incorporating African Next Voices / Swivuriso), and achieves **4.9% average CER on `xho_Latn`** at 7B parameters (`per_language_results_table_7B_llm_asr.csv`), with CTC checkpoints down to 325M parameters (`omniASR_CTC_300M_v2`).
- `[SOURCE]` **Community Hugging Face models** for isiXhosa remain sparse and mostly limited to older read-speech corpora: `BuzzASR/xhosa` (MIT, `whisper-large-v3` on FLEURS, **11.5% CER / 51.81% WER** on FLEURS), `TheirStory/whisper-small-xhosa` (Apache 2.0, NCHLT, 29.73% WER reported), and `misterkissi/w2v2-lg-xls-r-300m-xhosa` (CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0, 17.09% eval WER).

`[INFERENCE]` **Bottom line for Q1:** In late 2026, the problem is no longer "does any isiXhosa speech recognizer exist?" Commercial APIs (Azure, Google Chirp 2, ElevenLabs, Gemini) and Meta's open Omnilingual ASR already transcribe clean monolingual isiXhosa. The real, unaddressed gap is four-part:
1. `[INFERENCE]` **Everyday South African isiXhosa-English code-switching**, where speakers blend English technical and casual vocabulary with isiXhosa grammar inside fast, noisy conversation (and where neither commercial APIs nor open models publish strong, open-licence benchmarks).
2. `[INFERENCE]` **Small, offline, permissive-licence, edge-ready models** (<300-800 MB) that work privately on phones, browsers, and cheap laptops with zero per-minute cloud cost (essential for South African data costs, privacy on WhatsApp voice notes/clinics, and offline hardware like Siyamthanda).
3. `[INFERENCE]` **Standard orthography, punctuation, and product UX** (correction loops, pause-aware voice note transcription, and pairing ASR with strong downstream translation so the output is actually useful).
4. `[INFERENCE]` **Transparent, multi-domain evaluation** (read vs unscripted vs code-switched, with leak checks), because headline numbers from vendors and single-corpus academic splits are frequently misleading.

## 3. Question 2: Reasoning from First Principles, Was the Approach Right?

### 3.1 Technical concepts introduced here (plain English)

`[SOURCE]` **WER (Word Error Rate)** measures the percentage of words that must be inserted, deleted, or substituted to turn a model's output into the reference transcript; **CER (Character Error Rate)** does the same per letter (Radford et al., 2022; Barnard et al., 2014). An **agglutinative language** like isiXhosa builds long words by gluing prefixes, roots, and suffixes together, so a single wrong letter ruins an entire word under WER (`SPEC.html`, `README.md`).

`[SOURCE]` **Encoder-decoder (autoregressive) ASR** like Whisper turns audio into a **log-mel spectrogram** (a 2D time-by-frequency picture of sound), encodes a fixed 30-second window, and generates text one **subword token** at a time using a **BPE (Byte Pair Encoding) tokenizer** (a table that splits text into reusable chunks) (Radford et al., 2022). By contrast, **CTC (Connectionist Temporal Classification)** models like wav2vec 2.0, XLS-R, MMS, W2v-BERT 2.0, and Omnilingual CTC predict a letter or blank symbol directly for every short slice of audio (about every 20 ms) in parallel, with no autoregressive text generator (Pratap et al., 2023; Omnilingual ASR team, 2025). **Self-supervised learning (SSL)** means pretraining an audio encoder on millions of hours of *untranscribed* speech by predicting masked audio frames before fine-tuning on labeled transcripts (Seamless Communication, 2023; Omnilingual ASR team, 2025).

### 3.2 Base model choice: Whisper fine-tuning vs alternatives

`[INFERENCE]` **What was right (especially for a learning project):**
- `[INFERENCE]` Choosing Whisper (`small` 244M, `medium` 769M) was **the smartest entry point for learning and shipping a zero-cost demo**. Its Hugging Face training stack, CTranslate2 8-bit quantization (`ct2-int8`, shrinking weights from 16/32-bit floats to 8-bit integers for laptop CPU speed), and ONNX browser export (`transformers.js`) let the owner ship a working browser demo at $0 after Hugging Face gated free Docker Spaces (P-0014, D-0020, R-0017, R-0028).
- `[INFERENCE]` Whisper's **MIT license** avoids the **CC-BY-NC-4.0 non-commercial trap** of Meta's `facebook/mms-1b-all` (`huggingface.co/facebook/mms-1b-all`).
- `[INFERENCE]` Skipping NVIDIA's `canary-1b-v2` and `parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3` was **100% right**: both models only cover 25 European languages and have no African acoustic pretraining (`huggingface.co/nvidia/canary-1b-v2`, `huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3`).
- `[INFERENCE]` Doing **full fine-tuning** instead of **LoRA** (Low-Rank Adaptation, which freezes base weights and trains tiny adapter matrices) was **scientifically right for accuracy**. Qian et al. (2024, `arXiv:2407.06800`, Table 4) tested Whisper large-v3 on three unseen languages (Irish, Asturian, Sorani Kurdish) and proved full fine-tuning beat LoRA on every language (e.g., Irish 43.7% vs 54.8% WER; Sorani Kurdish 32.9% vs 41.3% WER).
- `[INFERENCE]` Once the project scaled past ~150-250 hours via Swivuriso, Whisper moved into its sweet spot. Both Nahabwe et al. (2025, `arXiv:2512.10968`) and the Swivuriso benchmark (Marivate et al., 2025/2026, `arXiv:2512.02201`, Table 9) show that at larger data budgets, Whisper matches or slightly outperforms CTC baselines in WER: on 7-language Swivuriso at 10,000 steps, `Whisper-large-v3-Turbo` reached **15% WER**, `Wav2Vec-BERT 2.0` reached **17% WER**, and adapter-only `MMS-1B-all` lagged at **32% WER**.

`[INFERENCE]` **What was wrong (for a fast, reliable, code-switched product):**
- `[INFERENCE]` **Fixed 30-second encoder bottleneck:** Whisper always pads audio to 30 seconds (`max_source_positions = 1500` in `openai/whisper-medium` `config.json`). In R-0026 (`runs/R-0026/run.md`), this meant **75% of laptop CPU time** (2.89 s out of 3.86 s for a 4.2 s clip) was spent encoding mostly silence, and shortening the window without retraining destroyed accuracy (P-0012; R-0026). Variable-length CTC encoders process only actual speech duration: in R-0032 (`runs/R-0032/run.md`), Meta Omnilingual CTC 1B ran **30% faster** on CPU than R-0019 `whisper-medium` (median 1.37 s vs 1.96 s) despite having more parameters (975M vs 769M).
- `[INFERENCE]` **Autoregressive hallucinations and repetition loops:** Because Whisper generates text token by token conditioned on its own prior output, a bad step can trigger infinite repetition or phantom words on silence. Barański et al. (ICASSP 2025, `arXiv:2501.11378`) showed non-speech audio systematically triggers Whisper hallucinations. This happened directly in this repo: R-0023 wrote `"i"`, `"c"`, or `"thetha"` on pure silence (`runs/R-0023/run.md`), and a **single 901-letter runaway loop** added 770 character errors and caused R-0029 to fail its ship rule (`runs/R-0029/run.md`). CTC models align monotonically to audio frames and cannot loop infinitely.
- `[INFERENCE]` **High tokenizer fertility on isiXhosa:** **Token fertility** is the average number of subword tokens needed to spell one word. BuzzASR (Singh et al., EMNLP 2026, `arXiv:2609.09554`, Table 6) measured Whisper's multilingual BPE tokenizer on isiXhosa at **3.44 tokens per word** (2.48 characters/token, only 6.0% vocabulary usage) versus **1.19 tokens per word** for English. Every isiXhosa word requires nearly 3x more sequential decoding steps, amplifying autoregressive error accumulation and hurting whole-word accuracy.
- `[INFERENCE]` **Zero isiXhosa pretraining and an overlooked 2025 alternative:** Whisper had zero isiXhosa supervision and only ~5.4 hours of Swahili in its 680,000-hour pretraining set (Nahabwe et al., 2025; Radford et al., 2022). Meanwhile, **W2v-BERT 2.0** (600M, MIT, pretrained on 4.5M hours across 143 languages *explicitly including `xh` and `zu`*, `huggingface.co/facebook/w2v-bert-2.0`) and **Meta Omnilingual ASR** (Apache 2.0, Nov 2025, `arXiv:2511.09690`, pretrained on 4.3M hours across 1,600+ languages and already fine-tuned on 598.8 h of isiXhosa) both existed before this project began in October 2026. Never testing a fine-tuned `w2v-bert-2.0` or `omniASR_CTC_300M_v2`/`1B_v2` against Whisper in Stage 3 was a genuine strategic gap.

### 3.3 Repurposing the `<|sw|>` Swahili language tag

`[SOURCE]` Qian et al. (2024, `arXiv:2407.06800`, Table 3) directly tested reusing an existing Whisper language tag (English, Welsh, Occitan, Spanish) versus adding a brand-new language token (`ga`) when fine-tuning Whisper large-v3 on an unseen language (Irish on FLEURS). After full fine-tuning, all reused tags and the new tag converged to **virtually identical scores**: 22.2%-22.6% CER (43.7%-44.7% WER) for reused tags versus 22.8% CER (44.5% WER) for the new token.

`[SOURCE]` However, Qian et al. (Table 5) also showed that full fine-tuning on a single new language causes severe **catastrophic forgetting** (destroying what the model knew before): the borrowed language's WER exploded from 27.5% to 96.8%, and even English degraded (4.1% to 6.1% on FLEURS, 5.6% to 13.0% on Artie).

`[REPO]` In this project (`decisions.md` D-0007, D-0015; `src/train.py` lines 66-68), reusing `<|sw|>` worked cleanly for monolingual isiXhosa and kept CTranslate2/ONNX conversion painless, but it overwrote Swahili, broke the `<|translate|>` task (D-0015: R-0012 still outputs isiXhosa when asked to translate), and forced inference into a single hardcoded `<|sw|>` mode.

`[INFERENCE]` **Verdict on `<|sw|>`:** Right for Stage 2 and monolingual accuracy (adding `<|xh|>` would not have meaningfully lowered monolingual CER/WER, based on Qian et al. Table 3). Wrong for a multilingual or code-switched product: forcing one language tag at every step fights mid-sentence switches into English. By contrast, Meta's Omnilingual ASR (`arXiv:2511.09690`, Table 22) found that conditioning on a language tag only **50% of the time during training** yields strong performance both with and without language tags at inference.

### 3.4 Data choices, training recipe, and model sizes

`[REPO]` **What was right in data progression:** Adding FLEURS (13 h) in R-0007 fixed the catastrophic word-gluing from NCHLT's short prompts (FLEURS WER dropped from 94.86% in R-0005 to 44.38% in R-0007, `results.md`). Adding Swivuriso short clips (146.6 h) in R-0019 cut unscripted speech CER by 62% (10.8% to 4.1%) and unexpectedly improved code-switched Soapies CER from 38.43% to 29.29% (`runs/R-0019/run.md`, `runs/R-0030/run.md`) because Swivuriso keeps spoken English words in English. Building an MMS **forced alignment** long-clip cutter (matching known transcript words to exact audio timestamps so long clips can be cut at natural pauses without slicing words in half) with a 600-clip quality gate (`src/swivuriso_long.py`, R-0027) cleanly unlocked 98.9 h of unscripted speech (83.7 h unscripted) and halved the browser demo model's unscripted CER (11.8% to 5.1%, `runs/R-0027/run.md`). Keeping research-only Soapies strictly for evaluation (D-0016) protected the open release cleanly.

`[SOURCE]` The Swivuriso paper (Marivate et al., 2025/2026, `arXiv:2512.02201`, Table 6) independently confirms this repo's central data lesson: a model trained only on NCHLT got 14% WER on NCHLT but collapsed to **71% WER** on Swivuriso (+57 points), whereas a model trained on Swivuriso got 24% on Swivuriso and 29% on NCHLT (+5 points).

`[REPO]` **Three major training recipe mistakes found by auditing `src/train.py` and run logs:**
1. `[REPO]` **Training budget starved as data scaled 6x:** `src/train.py` kept `--max-steps 4000` (batch 32 = 128,000 clip presentations) with linear learning-rate decay to 0. That gave ~2.8 epochs on 64.2 h (R-0012, `runs/R-0012/model/log_history.json`), but only **1.64 epochs** on 210.8 h (R-0019, `runs/R-0019/model/log_history.json`) and **1.30 epochs** on 309.7 h (R-0027 and R-0029, `runs/R-0029/model/log_history.json`). In both R-0019 and R-0029, NCHLT and FLEURS dev losses were still dropping cleanly at step 4000 when learning rate hit zero (e.g., R-0029 FLEURS dev loss fell continuously: 0.666 -> 0.517 -> 0.460 -> 0.437 -> 0.417 -> 0.392 -> 0.386 -> 0.382). This almost certainly suppressed R-0029's gain over R-0019.
2. `[REPO]` **Checkpoint selection biased toward legacy read speech:** `src/train.py` line 306 picked the best checkpoint using `nchlt_cer` (600 short NCHLT read clips) even when training on 245 h of Swivuriso. Swivuriso's own 25.2 h `dev` split was never used. In R-0027 (`runs/R-0027/train.log` line 1067; `runs/R-0027/model/log_history.json`), this literally caused the trainer to **throw away step 4000 and pick step 3500** (`nchlt_cer` 4.25 vs 4.29) even though `eval_fleurs_wer` improved from 38.12% to 37.58% and `eval_fleurs_loss` fell from 0.5005 to 0.4973 at step 4000!
3. `[REPO]` **Zero regularization or audio augmentation:** `openai/whisper-medium` defaults to `apply_spec_augment = None` (off), and `src/train.py` never enables **SpecAugment** (randomly masking bands of frequencies and time slices in the spectrogram during training, Park et al., 2019, `arXiv:1904.08779`, which OpenAI itself used for Whisper large-v2 in `arXiv:2212.04356`) nor adds background noise/speed perturbation, despite `SPEC.html` planning augmentation in Stage 3.

`[REPO]` **Text normalization trade-off (`src/scoring.py`, D-0010):** Converting hyphens to spaces (`e-Afrika` -> `e afrika`) and stripping all punctuation and capitals made training targets match scoring rules cleanly, which was great for fair experiments, **but hurts product usefulness**: standard isiXhosa orthography uses hyphens between prefixes and loanwords/proper nouns, and downstream translators like NLLB-200 expect punctuated, cased text (`runs/R-0032/run.md`).

`[INFERENCE]` **Model sizes (`small` 244M + `medium` 769M) were well chosen for the hardware:** On a 15 GB laptop CPU and a browser WASM worker, `small` (~300 MB ONNX int8) and `medium` (~742 MB CT2 int8) hit a realistic memory ceiling (P-0011, P-0013). Notably, jumping blindly to `openai/whisper-large-v3-turbo` (809M) would **likely worsen short-clip laptop CPU latency**: its `config.json` has **32 encoder layers at d_model=1280** (and 4 decoder layers), roughly doubling the encoder compute relative to `whisper-medium` (24 layers at d_model=1024), while R-0026 proved the encoder already accounts for 75% of CPU runtime! (Uncertain by exact milliseconds without running a local timing benchmark.)

### 3.5 Evaluation design, metrics (WER vs CER), and published comparisons

`[REPO]` **Evaluation hygiene was unusually strong - better than several published papers:** The project caught that **82% of NCHLT test clips** (2,259 of 2,770) repeat a training sentence (`decisions.md` note 2026-10-17, `runs/R-0004/run.md`) and immediately began reporting **unseen-sentence WER/CER** separately; it filtered 6,139 overlapping sentences out of Swivuriso training (`runs/R-0019/run.md`); it verified int8/ONNX accuracy against GPU fp16 before shipping (R-0013, R-0017, R-0020, R-0028, R-0031); it set pre-registered pass/ship rules; and it used paired bootstrap confidence intervals in R-0032.

`[SOURCE]` Reporting **both CER and WER** is strongly justified from first principles. In Barnard et al. (SLTU 2014, Table 4 and Table 6), NCHLT isiXhosa contains **29,130 distinct word types** across ~137k word tokens (with an **11.56% OOV, or out-of-vocabulary, rate** - words appearing in the test set that never appeared in training), compared to only **8,351 word types** across ~223k tokens in NCHLT English (0.97% OOV rate) - 3.5x more unique word forms in fewer total words.

`[REPO]` Codex's error recount on R-0021 (`docs/codex-ideas-2026-10-21.md`) reinforces this directly: **111 of 149 aligned word substitutions differed by only 1-2 character edits**, and only 8 of 200 clips differed solely in spacing. CER tracks acoustic and phonetic progress cleanly, while WER tracks whether words are actually spelled right for a reader or translator.

`[REPO]` **Two genuine flaws in evaluation:**
1. `[REPO]` **Unguarded greedy decoding in GPU evaluation (`src/baseline.py` line 58):** **Greedy decoding** picks the single highest-probability token at every step (faster, but prone to traps), whereas **beam search** (e.g., beam 5) tracks the 5 best partial sentences simultaneously. Evaluating with greedy decoding and no repetition/compression fallback allowed a single runaway clip (23.6 s -> 901 characters) to add 770 letter errors and reject R-0029 (`runs/R-0029/run.md`), even though OpenAI's own Whisper paper (Radford et al., 2022, `arXiv:2212.04356` Section 4.5) explicitly uses beam 5 plus gzip compression-ratio > 2.4 temperature fallback to stop greedy loops!
2. `[REPO]` **Small, narrow code-switched benchmark and minor non-standard test filtering:** Soapies test has only 5 TV characters and 14.3 minutes (`runs/R-0014/run.md`), and the owner's own everyday Cape Town recordings were never frozen into a dev/test set (`TODO.md`). Meanwhile, dropping 7 FLEURS clips >30 s (`runs/R-0019/eval_fleurs/metrics.json`) and 1,494 Swivuriso `dev_test` clips (>30 s or containing `[?]`, `runs/R-0019/run.md`) makes scores slightly optimistic relative to unfiltered external benchmarks.

## 4. Question 3: Concrete Steps That Would Most Reliably Improve the Project From Here

`[INFERENCE]` These steps are ranked strictly by **expected gain per dollar and per hour of effort**, respecting the constraints of tens of dollars in AWS credits, one GPU at a time, a 15 GB laptop CPU, and strict open-licensing rules.

### Step 1: Add Whisper decoding safety guards and Silero VAD (`$0`, ~2-4 hours)

`[SOURCE]` OpenAI's Whisper paper (Radford et al., 2022, `arXiv:2212.04356` Section 4.5) explicitly pairs beam 5 with a **gzip compression-ratio threshold of 2.4** and **temperature fallback** (retrying at temperatures 0.2, 0.4, ..., 1.0 when text loops or log-probability drops below -1.0), plus a `no_speech_threshold` of 0.6. The `faster-whisper` engine (`github.com/SYSTRAN/faster-whisper`) implements all of these out of the box alongside **Silero VAD** (Voice Activity Detection, which strips non-speech segments before encoding).

`[REPO]` Right now, `src/baseline.py` (line 58) uses bare greedy decoding with no fallback, and `src/live.py` (lines 106-124) hard-slices recordings at exactly 30.0 seconds with no VAD and no repetition fallback. That directly caused R-0029's 901-letter runaway (`runs/R-0029/run.md`) and R-0023's hallucinations on silence (`runs/R-0023/run.md`).

`[INFERENCE]` Moving both evaluation and `src/live.py` onto `faster-whisper` (already verified in `integrations/siyamthanda/` in R-0032!) with `vad_filter=True`, `compression_ratio_threshold=2.4`, temperature fallback, and pause-aware VAD chunking costs **$0** and immediately fixes silence hallucinations, mid-word 30 s cuts on WhatsApp voice notes, and runaway loops. Re-scoring R-0029 vs R-0019 with guarded decoding on CPU/GPU (~$0-$0.25) likely validates the already-paid-for R-0029 checkpoint immediately (capturing unscripted Swivuriso CER 4.1 -> 3.6% and Soapies CER 29.6 -> 28.1% for virtually free).

### Step 2: Benchmark Meta's Apache 2.0 `omniASR_CTC_300M_v2` and `1B_v2` against R-0019 on Swivuriso and Soapies (~$0.50, ~2-3 hours)

`[SOURCE]` Meta's December 2025 Omnilingual ASR v2 release (`github.com/facebookresearch/omnilingual-asr`) provides Apache 2.0 `omniASR_CTC_300M_v2` (325M params, 1.3 GiB FP32, ~96x relative GPU speed vs 7B LLM) and `omniASR_CTC_1B_v2` (975M params, 3.7 GiB FP32) trained across 1,600+ languages including 598.8 h of isiXhosa (`per_language_results_table_7B_llm_asr.csv`).

`[REPO]` In R-0032 (`runs/R-0032/run.md`), R-0019 beat Meta CTC 1B on 100 FLEURS clips (30.36% vs 35.97% WER), but `TODO.md` rightly noted that R-0019 had home-ground advantage on FLEURS style and that the two models were never compared on Swivuriso unscripted speech or Soapies code-switching.

`[INFERENCE]` Spend ~$0.50 on one spot CPU/GPU cloud run to measure R-0019, R-0029, `omniASR_CTC_300M_v2`, and `omniASR_CTC_1B_v2` head-to-head on Swivuriso `dev_test` and Soapies test (both accuracy and latency). This single cheap test tells you whether further Whisper investment beats a zero-effort Apache 2.0 CTC baseline - preventing wasted training runs.

### Step 3: Fix training under-budgeting, dev selection, and missing SpecAugment on the existing 310 h dataset (~$8-$12, ~4-6 hours)

`[REPO]` As shown in Section 3.4, R-0029 stopped at **1.30 epochs** while FLEURS dev loss was still falling (`runs/R-0029/model/log_history.json`), selected checkpoints on NCHLT dev CER (`src/train.py` line 306), and trained with `apply_spec_augment` off and zero audio noise/speed augmentation.

`[SOURCE]` OpenAI added SpecAugment, Stochastic Depth, and 2.5x longer training to move Whisper from large-v1 to large-v2 (`arXiv:2212.04356`). Park et al. (2019, `arXiv:1904.08779`) showed SpecAugment consistently prevents overfitting and improves robustness on noisy/unseen speech at essentially zero compute overhead.

`[INFERENCE]` Run one clean L40S experiment (~4-5 hours on `g6e.xlarge` at $1.861/h = ~$8-$10) on the existing 309.7 h dataset with four tightly coupled training fixes (or two sequential A/B runs if strict single-variable discipline is preferred): (a) train to **2.5-3 epochs** (~8,000-9,000 steps) with cosine LR decay; (b) select best checkpoint on **Swivuriso `dev` + FLEURS `dev` CER** (not NCHLT dev); (c) enable **`apply_spec_augment=True`**; and (d) add light room/background noise + 0.9x-1.1x speed perturbation. Expected gain: roughly **-0.5 to -1.5 CER points** on FLEURS and Swivuriso unscripted speech, plus better resilience to noisy/fast live speech (R-0024). (Uncertain exact magnitude until measured, because NCHLT+FLEURS+Swivuriso overfitting point on medium beyond 1.64 epochs has not been observed yet.)

### Step 4: Expose beam-5 word alternatives in Thetha and freeze a 30-50 clip everyday Cape Town mixed-speech benchmark (`$0`, ~2-3 hours)

`[REPO]` R-0022 (`runs/R-0022/run.md`) already proved that returning all 5 beam hypotheses costs **0.4% extra CPU time** (498.6 s vs 496.5 s) while containing **43% of missing words (36 of 83)** - dropping the best-of-5 ceiling WER from **30.38% to 18.09%** (-12.3 points). Meanwhile, `TODO.md` still waits on ~20-50 everyday founder sentences, leaving Soapies (5 TV drama actors) as the only mixed-speech test set.

`[INFERENCE]` Building the click-to-pick alternative word UI + audio replay in `src/live.html` gives immediate real-world utility for voice notes at $0 cloud cost AND turns every user test into a fast, low-friction way to build a genuine, consented Cape Town isiXhosa-English dev/test set (and later high-value training data).

### Step 5: Unlock remaining open isiXhosa and sister Nguni data (~$4-$8, ~4-6 hours)

`[REPO]` Two large open-licence pools are already sitting next to the pipeline: (1) **NCHLT isiXhosa Auxiliary** (~158 h CC BY 3.0, already downloaded once but blocked by a ZIP64 unpack error in P-0017, `problems.md`), and (2) **Swivuriso isiZulu (502.85 h)** and **isiNdebele (251.86 h)** (both CC BY 4.0, Nguni sister languages sharing click consonants, noun-class morphology, and South African English loanwords; `arXiv:2512.02201` Table 2), plus 1,498 Swivuriso long clips that `src/swivuriso_long.py` dropped solely because they contained digits (`runs/R-0027/run.md`).

`[SOURCE]` Two independent studies support sister-language transfer for South African ASR: Swivuriso's multilingual 7-language Whisper-large-v3-turbo run reached **15% WER** at 10,000 steps (`arXiv:2512.02201` Table 9), and Ògúnrèmí et al. (`arXiv:2311.15077` Table 3) proved pre-finetuning on the other three South African code-switched pairs cut `xho-eng` WER by **13.2 absolute points** (72.2% -> 59.0%). By contrast, BuzzASR (`arXiv:2609.09554` Section 5.3, Appendix F) found bilingual Whisper fine-tuning slightly hurt vs monolingual fine-tuning on small FLEURS pairs, so Nguni co-training must be tested strictly as a pre-training/stage-1 step followed by isiXhosa specialization (never assumed without validation!).

`[INFERENCE]` Unpack NCHLT auxiliary on a cheap CPU node with `bsdtar`/`7z` and run it through R-0027's exact R-0019 quality gate (discarding noisy/mismatched clips), and test Nguni co-training as a controlled experiment. Expected gain: moderate on read/general isiXhosa (-0.3 to -1.0 CER), uncertain on NCHLT auxiliary until its noise level is measured.

### Step 6: Redesign Stage 4 for real code-switching and safe hotword prompting (~$5-$8, ~3-5 hours)

`[REPO]` R-0015/R-0016 (`runs/R-0015/job.sh`) planned to continue from R-0012 by adding 10 h of NCHLT English (`<|en|>`) plus 8,000 stitched NCHLT isiXhosa+English clips. R-0023 (`runs/R-0023/run.md`) separately showed that passing a word list ("Thetha model twitter") as Whisper's previous-text prompt hurt WER by +1.4 to +1.7 points because R-0019 was never trained with prompts.

`[SOURCE]` Critically, Ògúnrèmí et al. (2023, `arXiv:2311.15077`, Table 3) proved that pre-finetuning on **monolingual read-speech NCHLT data actually worsened Soapies code-switched WER** (72.2% -> 77.5% on `xho-eng`) because of the domain gulf between studio read speech and spontaneous dialogue! Meanwhile, Omnilingual ASR (`arXiv:2511.09690`, Table 22) demonstrated that training with language-tag conditioning only **50% of the time** preserves accuracy when language identity is ambiguous or mixed.

`[INFERENCE]` Do NOT launch `runs/R-0015/job.sh` as written (it starts from obsolete R-0012 and stitches read-speech NCHLT clips). Instead, run a continuation from the best Swivuriso medium checkpoint that: (a) upsamples Swivuriso's natural **unscripted clips containing `[cs]` code-switches**; (b) stitches *unscripted* Swivuriso clauses with natural South African English speech (keeping cross-faded or short pauses, not studio single-word NCHLT clips); (c) drops or randomizes the language conditioning on a fraction of steps (or trains mixed clips with `<|sw|>` while preserving `<|en|>` on English clips); and (d) trains with Codex's prompt-robustness recipe (50% unprompted, 25% relevant rare/proper words from the clip, 25% unrelated distractors, prompt masked from loss) to fix "Thetha" -> "tether" without R-0023's +1.4 WER penalty.

`[REPO]` R-0026 (`runs/R-0026/run.md`) proved that trimming Whisper's encoder window to `max(10 s, audio + 1 s)` made `R-0019-int8` **3.1x faster on the owner's Ryzen 7 laptop** (4.67 s -> 1.50 s per clip), failing only because the model had never seen short windows in training (+1.2 WER on test, +3.1 on dev).

`[INFERENCE]` A short continuation (~1,000-1,500 steps, ~$3-$5 on L40S) that randomly varies encoder padding/window lengths between actual clip length (+1 s) and 30 s could recover nearly all of that accuracy while cutting laptop CPU latency by 2.5-3x. (Uncertain exact accuracy recovery on Whisper-medium without running the ablation, because Whisper's learned positional embeddings expect 1,500 frames; verify carefully on both short and 25-30 s clips.)

`[REPO]` R-0032 (`runs/R-0032/run.md`) proved that cutting isiXhosa WER from 35.97% (Meta 1B) to 30.36% (R-0019) barely moved English translation **BLEU** (a 0-100 score measuring word-sequence overlap against a reference translation: 18.08 -> 18.76, p = 0.19) because **NLLB-600M** hits a ~19-21 BLEU ceiling even on ground-truth unpunctuated isiXhosa text.

## 5. Question 4: Is There a Fundamentally Better Approach That Could Improve the Project Drastically?

### 5.1 First-principles diagnosis: what actually caps performance?

`[INFERENCE]` Before picking a new architecture, we must separate **what limits accuracy** from **what limits speed/reliability**:
1. `[INFERENCE]` **Data domain and volume (the primary accuracy ceiling):** Moving R-0012 (64 h) -> R-0019 (211 h) cut unscripted CER by 62% with zero architecture change (`runs/R-0019/run.md`). Conversely, BuzzASR (`arXiv:2609.09554`, Tables 7-8) used `whisper-large-v3` (2x larger than R-0019) on ~13 h of FLEURS+CV25 and got **51.72% WER**, worse than R-0019's **30.32% WER**. On Soapies code-switching, even 310 h of monolingual/read+unscripted isiXhosa still leaves **64.3% WER** (`results.md` R-0031), whereas training directly on ~14 h of in-domain South African code-switched speech + an n-gram LM reached **22.1% WER** (Ògúnrèmí et al., 2023, Table 6). **Takeaway: for code-switching, data domain is a bigger ceiling than model architecture.**
2. `[INFERENCE]` **Pretraining acoustic coverage + fixed 30 s encoder + autoregressive decoding (the primary speed, streaming, and hallucination ceiling):** Whisper had 0 h of isiXhosa pretraining, pads every clip to 30 s, and decodes 3.44 tokens per isiXhosa word sequentially with no monotonic frame lock.
3. `[INFERENCE]` **Tokenizer efficiency (a secondary, NOT primary, ceiling for Latin-script isiXhosa):** BuzzASR (`arXiv:2609.09554`, Tables 5-8) directly tested replacing Whisper's BPE tokenizer with a custom isiXhosa tokenizer: fertility dropped from **3.44 to 1.84 tokens/word** (1.87x compression), Latin-script decoder latency sped up **1.59x**, and Xhosa WER dropped **3.85 points** (51.72% -> 47.87%), while CER barely moved (11.48% vs 11.69%). Compare that to non-Latin Amharic in the same paper (fertility 11.74 -> 1.43, CER 49.39% -> 9.07%). Because isiXhosa uses the Latin alphabet and Whisper's encoder is 75% of laptop CPU time (R-0026), replacing Whisper's tokenizer alone gives only a modest ~4 WER gain and ~9% total laptop speedup - NOT a drastic breakthrough - while risking damage to Whisper's English vocabulary needed for code-switching.

### 5.2 Three fundamentally different paradigms compared against published evidence

#### Paradigm A: African-pretrained variable-length CTC/Transducer (`omniASR_CTC` or `w2v-bert-2.0`) + external code-switched n-gram LM

`[SOURCE]` **Published evidence:**
- `[SOURCE]` Meta's **Omnilingual ASR** (`arXiv:2511.09690`, Table 24) shows its 4.3M-hour self-supervised wav2vec 2.0 encoders (`OmniASR-W2V`, Apache 2.0) beat XLS-R and MMS across every benchmark, and `omniASR_CTC_300M_v2` (325M params) and `1B_v2` (975M params) run at **RTF (Real-Time Factor, compute time divided by audio length; lower is faster) of 0.001-0.002 on GPU (48-96x faster than 7B LLM)** with ~2-3 GiB VRAM (`github.com/facebookresearch/omnilingual-asr`).
- `[SOURCE]` **W2v-BERT 2.0** (600M, MIT, `huggingface.co/facebook/w2v-bert-2.0`) uses a **Conformer** encoder (combining convolutions for local sound patterns with attention for long-range context), was pretrained on 4.5M hours across 143 languages *including `xh` and `zu`*, and on 7-language Swivuriso (`arXiv:2512.02201`, Table 9) reached **17% WER** without an LM (just 2 points behind `whisper-large-v3-turbo`'s 15%).
- `[SOURCE]` **External n-gram Language Model (`KenLM`, a fast statistical table of common 3-to-5-word sequences) decoding on CTC** unlocks massive, cheap text-only corpora: in Nahabwe et al. (`arXiv:2512.10968`, Table 4), adding a 5-gram KenLM cut W2v-BERT WER on Luganda from **39.75% to 16.30%** and on Shona from **22.56% to 9.28%** (while NCHLT Xhosa moved more modestly from 7.13% to 6.67% because clean read speech was already saturated). In Ògúnrèmí et al. (`arXiv:2311.15077`, Table 6), adding a simple 3-gram KenLM to fine-tuned XLSR slashed Soapies `xho-eng` WER from **59.0% (greedy) to 22.1%**!
- `[SOURCE]` Longer term, **TDT (Token-and-Duration Transducer)** (Xu et al., 2023, `arXiv:2304.06795`) combines variable-length Conformer encoding with frame-skipping joint prediction, achieving streaming speed with stronger internal language modeling than plain CTC (used in NVIDIA Parakeet, though no African pretrained checkpoint exists yet).

`[INFERENCE]` **Expected size of gain:**
- `[INFERENCE]` **Speed and reliability (drastic):** 2-4x faster short-clip CPU latency than Whisper-medium, true real-time streaming capability (critical for a wearable/phone translator like Siyamthanda), and **0% infinite repetition loops**.
- `[INFERENCE]` **Accuracy (moderate to large if paired with a domain-matched code-switched KenLM):** Could push monolingual Swivuriso/FLEURS WER into the low-to-mid teens/twenties while letting you update vocabulary (names like "Thetha", Cape Town slang, code-switched bigrams) by rebuilding a 50 MB KenLM text file in minutes for $0 GPU!
- `[INFERENCE]` **Risks:** Without a well-matched LM, pure CTC often loses to Whisper on English loanwords and homophones (as seen in R-0032 where raw Meta 1B CTC lost to R-0019: 35.97% vs 30.36% WER); a poorly domain-matched LM can actually *hurt* WER (Nahabwe et al., 2025, Section 6.2 saw regressions on Bemba and Bambara); `fairseq2` has rougher Windows/browser ONNX support than Hugging Face Whisper.

#### Paradigm B: Speech Encoder + LLM Decoder (`omniASR_LLM_v2`, or Whisper/CTC + LLM N-best rescoring)

`[SOURCE]` **Published evidence:**
- `[SOURCE]` Meta's Omnilingual ASR (`arXiv:2511.09690`, Table 5) proves that swapping a CTC head for a Transformer/LLM decoder on top of the exact same wav2vec 2.0 speech encoder produces a **huge accuracy jump at every scale**: on FLEURS-81, 300M LLM-ASR achieves **7.8% CER vs 11.8% for 300M CTC** (-34% relative), and 1B LLM-ASR achieves **6.6% CER vs 8.6% for 1B CTC**.
- `[SOURCE]` At 7B scale, `omniASR_LLM_7B` hits **4.9% CER on `xho_Latn`** across Meta's evaluation suite (`per_language_results_table_7B_llm_asr.csv`), **6.62% CER / 35.41% WER** on BuzzASR's FLEURS+CV25 table (beating all other open baselines on Xhosa), and `omniASR_LLM_Unlimited_*_v2` removes the 40-second length limit (`github.com/facebookresearch/omnilingual-asr`).
- `[SOURCE]` Crucially, `omniASR_LLM` trained with **50% optional language conditioning** (`arXiv:2511.09690`, Table 22) does not force a single language tag, which structurally aligns with code-switching.

`[INFERENCE]` **Expected size of gain:**
- `[INFERENCE]` **Accuracy, code-switching, punctuation, and rare names (drastic):** A speech-LLM (or using an LLM to rescore R-0019's top-5 beam hypotheses, which R-0022 proved already contain 43% of missing words!) directly solves the "Thetha" vs "tether" and English-inside-isiXhosa ambiguity because an LLM knows world context, South African names, and both English and isiXhosa grammar.
- `[INFERENCE]` **Risks (severe for this project's hardware/budget):** Even the smallest `omniASR_LLM_300M_v2` is **1.63B total parameters** (6.1 GiB FP32, RTF 0.090 on an A100 GPU - ~90x slower than `omniASR_CTC_300M_v2`!), and `omniASR_LLM_7B_v2` is 7.8B params (30 GiB FP32). None of these run comfortably in a free browser Space or fast on a 15 GB laptop CPU. Hosting an API breaks the $0 serving rule. Also, unconstrained LLM post-editing can silently rewrite facts or hallucinate fluent nonsense (a risk Codex already flagged in `docs/codex-ideas-2026-10-21.md`).

#### Paradigm C: Teacher-Student Pseudo-Labeling + Distillation Data Engine (best strategic path under a $30-$100 budget)

`[SOURCE]` **Published evidence:**
- `[SOURCE]` **Distil-Whisper** (Gandhi, von Platen & Rush, 2023, `arXiv:2311.00430`) showed that pseudo-labeling large audio pools with a strong teacher, filtering strictly by a WER agreement heuristic, and distilling into a smaller decoder-pruned student achieves **5.8x faster inference with 51% fewer parameters** while staying within ~1% WER out-of-distribution.
- `[SOURCE]` **Biswas et al.** (LREC 2020, `arXiv:2003.03135`, Table 7) demonstrated on South African Soapies itself that semi-supervised pseudo-labeling of untranscribed broadcast speech reliably lowered code-switched English-isiXhosa WER (42.7% -> 41.0% bilingual; 54.5% -> 48.5% 5-lingual).

`[INFERENCE]` **Why this is the single highest-leverage "drastic" path for Ntlakanipho:**
1. `[INFERENCE]` Use **`omniASR_LLM_7B_v2` (Apache 2.0)** + **R-0019/R-0029** together as a **two-family teacher ensemble** on a rented L40S GPU (~$5-$10 for a few hours) to transcribe and agreement-filter:
   - `[INFERENCE]` The unpacked ~158 h NCHLT auxiliary audio (CC BY 3.0),
   - `[INFERENCE]` The remaining ~182 h of Swivuriso audio (recovering valid clauses around `[?]` spans and numeric utterances),
   - `[INFERENCE]` Any genuinely open-licensed (CC BY) or consented South African conversational/code-switched speech (e.g., consented student/community WhatsApp voice notes, Creative Commons South African podcasts/lectures/radio - strictly verifying licences, never scraping restricted broadcasts).
2. `[INFERENCE]` Keep **only segments where two architecturally distinct models agree closely** (e.g., `omniASR_LLM_7B_v2` and Whisper R-0019 agree within <= 5% CER). Cross-family agreement filters out both Whisper loops and CTC slips far better than single-model filtering!
3. `[INFERENCE]` Train a **fast, edge-ready student** on the combined clean + high-confidence pseudo-labeled pool: either **`omniASR_CTC_300M_v2` / `w2v-bert-2.0` + code-switched KenLM** (for real-time offline phone/laptop/wearable apps) OR a **variable-window Whisper student** (for the free in-browser ONNX demo).

`[INFERENCE]` **Expected gain:** Could realistically push monolingual FLEURS CER below **5%** (WER < 25%), cut Soapies / everyday code-switched WER by **15-25+ absolute points** (from ~65% toward ~40-50% without touching restricted Soapies training data), AND deliver **2-4x faster CPU/phone latency** with zero looping - all within ~$25-$40 of AWS credits. **Main risk:** Finding genuinely permissively licensed or consented *conversational code-switched* isiXhosa-English audio is a human/community effort, not a pure GPU problem; without in-domain code-switched audio, teacher pseudo-labeling only improves monolingual robustness.

- `[INFERENCE]` **Ògúnrèmí et al. (2023) trigram LM training split (`arXiv:2311.15077` Table 6):** Section 6 states the bigram and trigram KenLM models were trained "from each of the 4 language-pair datasets" without explicitly stating whether the Soapies test split transcripts were excluded from the n-gram LM; because the WER drop from 59.0% to 22.1% is unusually dramatic for a 3.14-hour corpus, treat that 22.1% number cautiously until the authors' code/splits are audited.
- `[INFERENCE]` **Nahabwe et al. (2025) NCHLT Xhosa split overlap (`arXiv:2512.10968` Table 3):** Section 5.1 states official splits were used where available and 5-hour stratified random splits otherwise; because their `whisper-small` 50 h Xhosa score (5.87% WER) is nearly 3x lower than Ntlakanipho's official 8-speaker NCHLT split (16.63% WER in R-0004), I could not verify whether their NCHLT Xhosa test split shared speakers or prompts with training.

(2025/2026) Table 10:** The paper specifies 16 kHz resampling, punctuation/tag removal, and 1-epoch training for Table 10, but does not state whether clips >30 s or `[?]` clips were excluded from test scoring in the same way `src/swivuriso.py` filtered 3,639 clips down to 2,145.
